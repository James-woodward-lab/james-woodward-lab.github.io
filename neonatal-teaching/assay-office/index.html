Measured or calculated?

Bicarbonate and base excess are calculated, not directly measured. The analyser measures pH and PaCO₂ first, then uses the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship for bicarbonate and a Siggaard–Andersen / Van Slyke-type equation for base excess.

Measured by electrodes and light

  • pHglass electrode
  • PaCO₂Severinghaus electrode
  • PaO₂Clark electrode
  • Hb, sO₂, COHb, MetHbco-oximeter

Bicarbonate calculation

The machine starts with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, written here with PaCO₂ in kPa:

pH = 6.1 + log10 [HCO₃⁻] 0.225 × PaCO₂

In mmHg the constant is 0.03: pH = 6.1 + log10([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PaCO₂)).

Rearranging gives the calculated bicarbonate:

[HCO₃⁻] = 0.225 × PaCO₂ × 10(pH − 6.1)

mmHg form: [HCO₃⁻] = 0.03 × PaCO₂ × 10(pH − 6.1). Bicarbonate comes out in mmol/L either way.

Worked example

pH 7.20, PaCO₂ 9.33 kPa (70 mmHg)

[HCO₃⁻] = 0.225 × 9.33 × 10(7.20 − 6.1) = 2.10 × 101.10 = 2.10 × 12.6 ≈ 26.4 mmol/L

So the analyser reports a bicarbonate of about 26 mmol/L. The high PaCO₂ has driven the acidaemia while bicarbonate is near normal: an acute respiratory acidosis.

Base excess calculation

Base excess asks: how much strong acid or base would need to be added to return the blood to pH 7.40, at PaCO₂ 5.3 kPa (40 mmHg) and 37 °C? A negative value is often called a base deficit.

Simplified formula

BE = 0.9287 × ([HCO₃⁻] − 24.4 + 14.83 × (pH − 7.40))

Or, directly from the two measured values:

BE = 0.209 × PaCO₂ × 10(pH − 6.1) + 13.77 × pH − 124.58

mmHg form: BE = 0.02786 × PaCO₂ × 10(pH − 6.1) + 13.77 × pH − 124.58. These are Siggaard–Andersen-derived approximations; exact algorithms vary slightly between analyser manufacturers.

Worked example

pH 7.18, HCO₃⁻ 11 mmol/L

BE = 0.9287 × (11 − 24.4 + 14.83 × (7.18 − 7.40)) = 0.9287 × (−13.4 + 14.83 × (−0.22)) = 0.9287 × (−13.4 − 3.26) ≈ −15.5 mmol/L

A base deficit of about 15 mmol/L: a substantial metabolic acidosis. The standard base excess formula below gives about −17.4 for the same gas, which is why the two figures on a printout rarely match exactly.

Why haemoglobin matters

Full blood base excess also accounts for haemoglobin, because haemoglobin is an important non-bicarbonate buffer. A more complete Van Slyke form is:

BE = (1 − 0.014 × Hb) × ([HCO₃⁻] − 24.8 + (1.43 × Hb + 7.7) × (pH − 7.40))

Hb is in g/dL here. UK results report Hb in g/L: divide by 10. The analyser may use measured haemoglobin from the co-oximeter to refine this calculation.

Standard versus actual base excess

Actual base excess ABE, BE(B)

What it represents: base excess of the blood sample at its actual haemoglobin and oxygenation.

Practical use: more influenced by the sample’s own haemoglobin buffering; useful when Hb is far from normal.

Standard base excess SBE, BE(ecf)

What it represents: base excess calculated for extracellular fluid, effectively assuming a normal (diluted) haemoglobin of about 5 g/dL.

Practical use: commonly used to estimate the metabolic component of an acid–base disorder.

Standard base excess is often calculated as:

SBE = [HCO₃⁻] − 24.8 + 16.2 × (pH − 7.40)

It is designed to isolate the non-respiratory, metabolic component more cleanly than actual base excess.

Normal acid–base.

Saloon version

  • Bicarbonate is the amount of “buffer barrels” currently in the saloon, calculated from the pH telegraph and the CO₂ still.
  • Base excess is the number of barrels of acid or base the sheriff would need to add, or remove, to restore the saloon to neutral pH while holding the CO₂ steam pressure constant.
  • Negative base excess / base deficit means the saloon has used up buffer stock: think metabolic acidosis.
  • Positive base excess means there is surplus buffer stock: think metabolic alkalosis.

Why the numbers can differ

  • Manufacturers’ algorithms vary. Two analysers given the same pH and PaCO₂ can print base excess figures a unit or so apart. Compare trends on the same machine.
  • Calculated HCO₃⁻ is not laboratory total CO₂. The lab’s “bicarbonate” on a biochemistry profile is measured enzymatically as total CO₂ and includes dissolved CO₂ and carbamino compounds, so it runs 1–2 mmol/L higher.
  • Temperature. Everything is measured at 37 °C. Entering a patient temperature (a cooled baby at 33.5 °C) changes the reported PaCO₂ and PaO₂ but not the calculated base excess.
  • Sample errors feed straight into the arithmetic. An air bubble lowers PaCO₂, so the calculated bicarbonate falls too. Liquid heparin dilutes the sample. A long delay lets cells make acid.
  • Hb matters for actual BE. At Hb 80 g/L the Van Slyke correction is small; at 220 g/L in a polycythaemic newborn it is not. Standard BE sidesteps this by assuming a fixed Hb.

Teaching aid only. Constants are the commonly quoted approximations and are not those of any particular analyser.
